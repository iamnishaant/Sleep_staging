# Compact Sleep Staging by Knowledge Distillation: A Leakage-Corrected Re-Evaluation

**Author:** Nishant Shah · Project 48 — Explainable Deep Learning for Sleep Disorder Detection
**Dataset:** Sleep-EDFx (PhysioNet) · **Revised:** 8 September 2026

---

## Abstract

This project set out to compress a sleep-staging model — a network that labels every 30 seconds of overnight EEG as Wake, N1, N2, N3 or REM — into a version small enough to train and run on free-tier cloud hardware, using knowledge distillation.

Before compressing anything, I audited the evidence behind the model I had inherited. It reported a Cohen's kappa of 0.6663, but that figure had been produced by dividing the data by recording rather than by subject. Because Sleep-EDFx records two nights per person, the same individuals appeared on both sides of the split. Re-measuring the identical checkpoint on a subject it had genuinely never encountered, its kappa fell to 0.5066 and its ability to identify deep sleep collapsed to zero — it had memorised individuals rather than learned sleep physiology. I therefore rebuilt the splits at subject level, holding out fifteen subjects that were loaded exactly once, at the very end, and never used for tuning or model selection.

I then retrained the teacher from scratch under the corrected protocol, and diagnosed a defect in its loss function that applied class weights twice over, suppressing the most common sleep stage; correcting it raised held-out performance substantially without adding any capacity. Next I designed a compact student, implemented the full distillation pipeline — soft-target loss with temperature scaling, cached teacher outputs, and unit tests covering the gradient correction that fails silently when omitted — and trained the student from two teachers of different quality, each run against an identical control trained on hard labels alone.

Both distilled students trained correctly and imitated their teachers closely, yet both were outperformed by the same architecture trained without distillation. Tracing the cause gave the project's first transferable finding: **a teacher must exceed the student's own undistilled performance before its guidance carries any usable information.** The student had outgrown its teacher during training, so half of every gradient was pulling a good model back toward a worse one. That is not a defect of the loss, the temperature, or the mixing weight, and no amount of tuning any of them would have helped.

A second audit then overturned one of my own conclusions. An ablation had shown that feeding the network the raw EEG waveform, rather than 34 precomputed band-power features, changed nothing — from which I had concluded the waveform carried no additional information. Measuring the encoder's receptive field by backpropagation showed why that conclusion was unsafe: it spanned **25 samples, a quarter of a second**, before averaging across the full 30-second epoch. No sleep spindle, K-complex, slow wave or sawtooth wave fits inside a quarter-second, so the experiment had measured the encoder rather than the signal. Replacing it with a two-branch encoder spanning 8.75 seconds moved the raw EEG from contributing nothing to driving 71% of predictions, raised N1 F1 by 0.092, and produced a student that beats every honestly-evaluated teacher in the project.

With a student that good, distillation became possible for the first time — not by training a larger teacher, but by averaging three existing models of comparable quality into an ensemble that exceeded any of its members. Diversity of input and architecture mattered far more than random seeds: adding a model that was 0.037 kappa *weaker*, but structurally different, tripled the ensemble's advantage. Distilling that ensemble into a single student removed the penalty entirely, but the gain did not appear where I had been watching. On accuracy the soft-label student ties its hard-label twin. What improved, consistently across both evaluation splits, was **calibration**: expected calibration error fell by a third, the error on REM latency — a clinically consequential quantity — nearly halved, and the night-level reliability signal separated good nights from bad ones 43% more sharply.

The delivered model is that distilled student: **139,606 parameters against the original 649,229, reaching kappa 0.7001 (95% CI 0.6432–0.7514) on fifteen unseen subjects** — 4.65× smaller than the model it replaces, and 0.09 kappa above the best teacher it was built to imitate. Alongside it I ship a per-night evidence packet in which every derived clinical measurement travels with its own measured error bound; only three of twenty-four survive as safe to assert without qualification, and reporting that honestly is part of the result rather than a shortfall.

Three further results came from taking the evaluation itself seriously. First, replacing the single validation split with five-fold subject-level cross-validation over the eighty-five non-test subjects showed that the split every experiment had been judged on was an unrepresentative draw: the same architecture scores kappa 0.7143 averaged across folds against 0.6763 on that one split, with all five folds above it. Past comparisons are unaffected, since both sides always faced the same yardstick, but every absolute statement about clearing a bar was flattering the bar. The across-fold standard deviation of 0.0193 kappa is now the project's working resolution, and for scale the entire distillation effect on kappa was 0.0008.

Second, although external validation is out of scope, Sleep-EDFx is not one cohort but two — a cassette study of healthy ageing and a telemetry study of subjects reporting mild difficulty falling asleep, half of them on temazepam, recorded on different hardware. Training on sixty-two cassette subjects and scoring on all twenty-two telemetry subjects, none ever seen, gives **kappa 0.5516 (95% CI 0.4751-0.6205) against 0.7384 on held-out cassette data: a transfer gap of 0.187**, nearly twice what the six telemetry recordings in the main test split had suggested. Reported alone that reads as a failure to generalise. Decomposed, it is more specific and more useful: recall is uniform across Wake, N2, N3 and REM at 67 to 72 per cent, and what collapses is Wake *precision*, from an in-cohort F1 of 0.898 to 0.622. Sleep-stage discrimination largely survives the cohort boundary; one class's behaviour does not.

Third, the obvious explanation for that — the cohorts differ threefold in how much wake they contain — is wrong, and I was able to show it rather than assume it. Correcting the model's outputs with *perfect* knowledge of the target cohort's class priors recovers 1.2 per cent of the gap; unsupervised prior estimation makes matters worse and misplaces N1 by nineteen percentage points, which is what that estimator does when its assumption of unchanged class-conditional distributions fails. The gap is therefore covariate shift, not prior shift: telemetry epochs genuinely look different rather than merely occurring in different proportions. The leading candidate was then tested rather than left standing: a per-recording-normalised copy of the dataset, with the 34 spectral features rescaled analytically to match, removes the twofold amplitude difference and makes transfer *worse* — a paired change of −0.0175 kappa, 95% confidence interval [−0.0318, −0.0033], with the cost falling hardest on the very class the correction was meant to repair. Both cheap explanations for the cohort gap are therefore measured and excluded, each with a quantitative bound rather than a failed attempt. What has shifted remains unidentified; recorder hardware, montage, medication, age and the differing shape of a lab night against a nine-hour home recording all remain live.

Because the project is nominally about *explainable* deep learning, the attribution claim was pre-registered before it was computed: five per-stage predictions, a pass criterion, and a void condition fixed in advance, because an attribution profile can be narrated as physiologically sensible after the fact almost regardless of what it says. Integrated Gradients satisfied its completeness axiom to within 0.03 per cent against a 5 per cent void threshold, and three of the five predictions held, including the one made a gating requirement. Two caveats keep that pass from being over-read, and both travel inside the shipped packet rather than only in the report. All five stages share the *same* top three features in different orders, so the per-stage predictions are individually true and jointly far weaker than they read: what distinguishes stages is the magnitude of shared features, not the choice of them, and the registration did not test that. And the branch split is dimension-biased — the engineered features take 18.7 per cent of attribution mass, which reads as a minor contribution until one notes it is spread over 34 dimensions against 3,000 raw samples, roughly twenty times the attribution per dimension. Both framings are arithmetically correct and they support opposite sentences, so both are stated and neither is asserted alone.

The same packet ships a per-epoch confidence flag for N1, the model's weakest and representation-bound class, where no decision threshold moves F1 by more than 0.0086. The threshold was fitted on validation and applied to the held-out split exactly once. The result is worth reporting precisely because it is split: the separation transfers — flagged N1 calls are right 33.7 per cent of the time against 44.7 per cent for unflagged ones, a gap of 10.9 points with a subject-clustered interval of [7.1, 15.8] that excludes zero — but the *level* does not, since validation had promised 61 per cent for the unflagged group and the 60 per cent target that selected the threshold is missed on held-out data. The flag is therefore documented as a ranking rule and not a level guarantee. This is the cross-validation finding above running in the opposite direction: one split was pessimistic about overall kappa and optimistic about an N1 sub-population, and neither bias was visible from that split alone.

Two limitations bound what any of this can claim. Every figure rests on a single dataset, and the cohort-transfer result above is a warning about exactly that: a model can lose 0.187 kappa crossing between two studies inside one corpus, so no clinical claim survives without genuine external validation. And the held-out set of fifteen subjects yields a confidence interval of roughly ±0.054 kappa — resampling subjects rather than recordings, because the two nights of one person are not independent evidence — which means the last several experiments returned ties not because the changes did nothing but because the evaluation can no longer resolve them — a constraint that further work on this dataset cannot lift.
